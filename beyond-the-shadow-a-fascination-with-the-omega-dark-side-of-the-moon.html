Beyond the Shadow: A Fascination with the Omega Dark Side of the Moon

Rishabh Saxena April 2026 5 min read

I’ve always been drawn to black watches. There’s a certain stealthy, versatile coolness to them—something understated but quietly confident. Over time, that preference started shaping my watch collection, almost without me realizing it.

My journey into watches actually began with the Casio G-Shock GBA-400-1A9, part of Casio’s G’Mix lineup introduced around 2015. It’s a hybrid analog-digital watch with Bluetooth connectivity and a rotary control that can manage music playback from a smartphone. Like most G-Shocks, it’s built around durability first—shock resistance, 200 meters of water resistance, and a highly legible dial. The black-and-gold color scheme gave it a bold personality and introduced me to the appeal of darker watch aesthetics.

As I started exploring mechanical watches, the industry itself seemed to be shifting toward interesting materials in more accessible segments. For instance, the Casio Edifice EFK-100XPB, released around 2025, represents an unusual step for the Edifice line. Traditionally known for quartz and solar chronographs, this model introduced a mechanical automatic movement alongside a forged-carbon dial and an exhibition caseback. It’s a sign that brands are experimenting with materials and mechanics even in categories where they historically did not.

Another interesting entry in this space is the Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 Carbon (T137.907.97.201.00). The PRX has already become one of the most recognizable integrated-bracelet watches of the past few years, and the carbon variant pushes it further with a forged carbon case. Inside is the Powermatic 80, a modern automatic movement developed within the Swatch Group ecosystem. It offers an impressive 80-hour power reserve, largely achieved by lowering the beat rate to 21,600 vibrations per hour and optimizing energy efficiency. While it may not carry the same finishing prestige as higher-end Swiss calibres, it’s undeniably practical: reliable, efficient, and designed for everyday wear.

My most recent watch, however, is the Seiko SRPD79K1, part of the Seiko 5 Sports “Street Style” series introduced in 2019. It runs on Seiko’s 4R36 automatic movement, a workhorse calibre known for reliability rather than decorative finishing. The movement offers hand-winding capability, hacking seconds, and around 41 hours of power reserve—features that make it a solid entry point into mechanical watchmaking. It’s not meant to compete with Swiss chronometers in terms of refinement, but that’s not the point. The charm of Seiko movements has always been their rugged dependability. They’re honest, durable engines built to run for years with minimal fuss.

But as much as I appreciate watches like the Seiko 5 or the PRX, my fascination with dark watches inevitably leads to a more ambitious destination: the Omega Speedmaster Dark Side of the Moon.

The Speedmaster Legacy

To understand the Dark Side of the Moon collection, it helps to start with the watch that made the Speedmaster famous: the Omega Speedmaster Professional Moonwatch.

The Speedmaster was officially qualified by NASA in 1965 after surviving a series of brutal tests designed to simulate the conditions of spaceflight—extreme temperatures, shock, vacuum exposure, and vibration. Four years later, during the Apollo 11 Moon Landing in 1969, astronaut Buzz Aldrin wore his Speedmaster on the lunar surface, making it the first watch worn on the Moon.

Few watches can claim such direct involvement in human history.

Nearly half a century later, Omega reimagined this heritage through modern materials when it launched the Dark Side of the Moon collection in 2013.

Ceramic and Modern Speedmaster Engineering

The original Dark Side of the Moon reference (Ref. 311.92.44.51.01.003) introduced a fully black zirconium-oxide ceramic case measuring 44.25 mm. Ceramic is significantly harder and more scratch-resistant than steel, though it is more complex to manufacture. Omega mills and sinters these cases with remarkable precision, creating surfaces that alternate between polished and satin finishes.

Inside the watch sits the Co-Axial Calibre 9300, one of Omega’s most important modern chronograph movements.

This is where Omega really distinguishes itself.

The 9300 is an in-house automatic chronograph with a column-wheel architecture and vertical clutch, a configuration prized for smooth chronograph engagement and improved durability. The movement also features twin barrels providing around 60 hours of power reserve, along with Omega’s Co-Axial escapement, originally developed by the legendary watchmaker George Daniels. The Co-Axial system reduces friction in the escapement and allows longer intervals between servicing compared to traditional Swiss lever escapements.

In other words, Omega movements aren’t just historically significant—they’re technically ambitious.

Later Dark Side models continued with variations of this architecture, including Calibre 9301, while more recent developments within Omega’s lineup have incorporated Master Chronometer certification, ensuring resistance to magnetic fields up to 15,000 gauss and strict chronometric testing by METAS.

For anyone who appreciates mechanical watchmaking, Omega’s modern movements represent a fascinating blend of innovation and heritage.

The Apollo 8 Variant

Among the Dark Side family, one model stands apart in both design and philosophy: the Omega Speedmaster Apollo 8 Dark Side of the Moon.

First introduced in 2018 (Ref. 311.92.44.30.01.001), the watch commemorates the Apollo 8 mission, the first crewed spacecraft to orbit the Moon in 1968.

Unlike most Dark Side models, the Apollo 8 returns to a manual-winding movement, originally the skeletonized Calibre 1869. In 2024, Omega updated the model with Calibre 3869, a hand-wound Co-Axial Master Chronometer movement derived from the modern Moonwatch architecture.

The movement’s bridges are laser-ablated to resemble the rugged surface of the Moon, showing the near side on the dial side and the far side on the caseback.

It’s one of the most visually distinctive Speedmaster movements ever made.

My Favorite Dial: The Pure “Black Black”

Despite the technical beauty of the Apollo 8 variant, the Dark Side of the Moon model that speaks to me the most is actually the “Black Black” edition.

The Omega Speedmaster Dark Side of the Moon Black Black, introduced around 2015, pushes the monochrome concept to its extreme. The dial, sub-dials, hands, and ceramic case are all rendered in deep black tones, with only subtle Super-LumiNova accents providing contrast.

Some collectors find it too subdued. For me, that’s exactly the point.

Most chronographs try to be visually busy—multiple colors, contrasting sub-dials, polished markers. The Black Black does the opposite. It embraces restraint. In bright light, the layered textures of the dial slowly reveal themselves. In dim environments, the lume emerges quietly from the darkness.

It’s not a watch that demands attention. It’s one that rewards attention.

Storytelling Through Watchmaking

One of the most charming examples of storytelling in the Speedmaster family is the Omega Speedmaster Silver Snoopy Award 50th Anniversary.

The watch commemorates the Apollo 13 mission, when NASA astronauts used a Speedmaster chronograph to time a critical engine burn that helped bring the damaged spacecraft safely back to Earth.

Its caseback features a mechanical animation: when the chronograph runs, a tiny spacecraft travels across the back of the watch, orbiting the Moon.

It’s playful, mechanical, and deeply tied to the Speedmaster’s heritage.

If I could imagine a similar storytelling moment for the Dark Side of the Moon, it might draw inspiration from Chandrayaan-3, through which the Indian Space Research Organisation achieved the first successful landing in the lunar south-polar region in 2023.

Imagine a caseback animation showing Chandrayaan’s descent toward the Moon—an elegant tribute connecting modern exploration with the Speedmaster’s long association with space.

Beyond the Shadow

Watches like the Seiko 5 Sports or the Tissot PRX remind me how accessible mechanical watchmaking has become. They offer dependable movements and thoughtful design at approachable prices.

But when you look at something like the Omega Speedmaster Dark Side of the Moon, you begin to see what happens when a brand pushes both engineering and storytelling further.

It’s not just a black ceramic chronograph. It’s a watch that connects material innovation, mechanical craftsmanship, and one of the most remarkable chapters in human exploration.

And for someone who has always been drawn to black watches, it feels like the natural summit of that fascination.

The darker the watch, it seems, the deeper the story behind it.
